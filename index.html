What the envelope means

The near end is planetary. It is the moment when the surface stopped being hostile to any chemistry at all, and nothing about biology sets it. The far end is biological. It is the latest moment by which LUCA must already have existed, and it depends entirely on which evidence you accept. The difference between the two is the whole budget the chemistry has to work in.

Chemistry becomes a probability

Each rung has a transfer time and a loss time. Their ratio is the Damkohler number, Da = tau loss divided by tau transfer. The probability that a species is handed on rather than destroyed is p = Da divided by (1 + Da). That is not an extra assumption on top of the handoff criterion. It is the handoff criterion written as a number between zero and one, so that it can be multiplied across rungs and turned into a waiting time.

A surface holds, protects and lets go

Between S2 and S4 most molecules are not floating free. They sit on a mineral. In the model a surface does three things. It holds a molecule, which is binding affinity. It protects it while it is held, which is stability, so the loss time grows. And it lets it go again, or does not, which decides whether the molecule can still meet a partner and react. Holding helps S2 and S3. For S4 there is a best middle: a surface that holds too tightly, or is already full, gathers molecules but stops them from reacting.

Phosphate is the model molecule. Nucleotides and DNA bind minerals mainly through their phosphate groups, so phosphate is a fair stand-in for anything with a phosphate backbone or a negative charge. The mechanisms follow the work of Karina K. Sand and co-workers at GLOBE: positive surfaces such as iron oxides, carbonates, clay edges and brucite-like layers bind directly, negative surfaces bind only through Mg2+ or Ca2+ bridges, steps and edges bind more tightly than flat faces, and very strong binding can damage what it holds.

Figure M1. How strongly each surface holds phosphate, and how that changes with pH

Distribution coefficient for each surface

Kd is how many litres of water one gram of mineral can strip of phosphate. Multiply it by the grams of mineral per litre: above 1, most of the phosphate sits on the surface. Oxides, green rust and clay edges lose their positive charge as pH rises, so they hold less. Quartz, glass and mica faces are negative throughout and hold phosphate only through Mg2+ and Ca2+ bridges, so their curves are low and flat. Brucite stays positive to about pH 11.

Figure M2. Holding helps, until it hinders

What a surface does to S2 and S4 as binding gets stronger

Lines are one generic surface whose affinity is swept from weak to strong, using the site density and locked fraction of the highlighted mineral. Points are the real minerals at your conditions (circles S2, diamonds S4). They sit off the lines because each mineral has its own site density, surface area and locked fraction. Protection (S2) rises as more is held, then falls once binding is strong enough to strain the molecule. The gain in activation speed (S4) falls when the surface fills up or holds its molecules too tightly to let them react.

T2 and T3 up close

T1 and T4 are measured in millions of years, so on most figures T2 and T3 shrink to nothing. That hides where the filtering happens. T2 is the chemistry: on every rung a molecule has to be passed on before it falls apart, and the time that takes is minutes to hours. T3 is the waiting for luck: if only some tries get through every rung, the world has to keep trying, cycle after cycle, at every site at once. This tab puts both on their own clock, in seconds, hours and years, so you can see how a change in a chance of passing turns into time.

Figure Z1. One ruler for all four terms

Every scenario, every term, in seconds on a powers-of-ten scale

On a scale where each step is ten times longer, minutes and millions of years fit on one line. With the default chemistry T2 and T3 sit between seconds and hours, ten to fifteen powers of ten below T1 and T4. Load the hard case and watch T3 jump to the right: the chemistry did not get slower, the chance of success got smaller.

Figure Z2. Inside T2, the race on each rung

Transfer time against loss time

The chance of passing a rung is p = Da / (1 + Da), where Da is the loss time divided by the transfer time. A rung where the rust bar is a hundred times longer than the teal bar passes 99 times in 100. The strip at the bottom shows the transfer times end to end in ordinary clock time, which is T2.

Figure Z3. The filter

How many tries are left after each rung

The chances multiply. Five rungs that each pass 9 times in 10 let through 6 tries in 10. Five rungs that each pass 1 time in 100 let through one try in ten billion. What is left at the end is the joint p that sets T3.

Figure Z4. How T3 grows as the chance shrinks

One line per scenario. More sites move a line down, longer cycles move it up

Each line is a straight slope: ten times smaller p means ten times longer T3. The lines differ only in how many sites try at once and how long one cycle lasts. The dotted drop line marks the joint p at which T3 alone would use up the whole budget for the highlighted scenario. Anything to the right of it leaves time to spare.

How far could the chance fall

The joint p at which T3 reaches a day, a year, a million years and the budget

The column "room" is how many powers of ten the joint p could still fall before T3 alone fills the budget. Large room means the outcome does not hang on the probability estimates. Small or negative room means it does.
